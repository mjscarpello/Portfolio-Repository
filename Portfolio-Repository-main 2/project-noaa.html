THE BUSINESS QUESTION

Frequency isn't the same as risk.

Weather forecasting can tell us whether a storm is likely to occur, but recorded storm characteristics do not directly tell us how much economic harm that event will cause. We wanted to understand whether the information already recorded by NOAA contains meaningful predictive signal about any reported property or crop damage.

That distinction matters for planning: a frequently occurring storm category is not necessarily the category responsible for the most damaging outcomes. The charts below show why our team approached this as an impact-prediction problem rather than a frequency forecast.

METHODS & REASONING

How we tested the idea.

01 / DATA

Define the target carefully

The binary outcome asked whether any recorded property or crop damage was associated with a storm. Events without a known economic outcome were not simply treated as zeros.

02 / FEATURES

Use characteristics, not answers

We focused on storm event type, geographic region, season, and storm duration. Columns describing outcomes after damage were not appropriate predictors.

03 / VALIDATION

Split by storm episode

Multiple records from the same storm system can be highly similar. Grouping the split by episode reduced the chance of related events appearing in both training and testing.

04 / EVALUATION

Compare two approaches

Logistic Regression provided an interpretable reference and Random Forest modeled nonlinear patterns. We emphasized Average Precision alongside ROC-AUC because the positive class was roughly 23%.

WHAT I TOOK AWAY

A model's performance is only one part of the answer.

Our team's results showed that event type carried a strong predictive signal, with additional context from region, duration, and season. Random Forest slightly outperformed the simpler Logistic Regression model in the reported comparison, but the model's association with damage does not establish why losses occur.

What stood out to me was the importance of validation choices. We could not assume that every record was a wholly independent weather event, and we could not treat an unknown outcome as though nothing happened. Both decisions affect what a model learns and how confidently its results can be interpreted.

The next major improvement would be to connect storm characteristics with exposure and vulnerability information, such as where people and infrastructure are located. NOAA's recorded outcomes also reflect differences in reporting practices, so predictions should never be treated as definitive measurements of real-world harm.
