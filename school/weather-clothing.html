Weather-Based Delivery Clothing Predictor.

A frustrating spring delivery shift inspired my first predictive analytics project. I wanted to use weather, route conditions and eventually personal experience to make better clothing decisions before work.

01 / THE ORIGIN

Prepared for winter. Working in spring.

The project started with a problem I experienced firsthand as an Amazon delivery driver.

Before the shift

Winter had become my normal.

I wore heavy wool snow pants and dressed for a cold day, as I had grown accustomed to doing throughout winter.

During the shift

The weather changed.

The sun came out, the day grew warm, and repeated trips in and out of the van made me even hotter. I had no suitable change of clothes, and the discomfort affected my focus and mood.

Another unpredictable day

I recognized a pattern.

When I faced a similar uncertainty about warmth and rain, I began to wonder how I could use data to make a more informed decision rather than repeatedly guessing.

The motivation: the first experience was emotional, but recognizing the recurring problem is what turned it into a data science project.

02 / THE PROBLEM

A forecast is not a clothing decision.

Weather information alone does not account for the way a particular driver experiences a particular route.

Weather exposure

Conditions change over a shift.

A cold morning can become a warm afternoon. Rain and other changes may make an additional layer or spare clothing useful.

Route & exertion

The work changes how it feels.

Stop frequency, repeated entry and exit, physical activity and changes in van temperature can influence comfort beyond the forecast.

Individual response

Drivers are different.

Personal heat and cold tolerance, available clothing and past experiences matter. I envisioned a driver profile rather than one fixed outfit for everyone.

I wanted to support comfort and preparedness because I believe they matter to safe, focused work. The prototype did not measure or demonstrate a safety benefit.

03 / PREDICTIVE ANALYTICS

From an ambitious idea to a testable prototype.

This was my first substantial predictive analytics project. Learning to define a feasible prediction problem was as important as training a model.

Original ambition

Personalized clothing decisions

Use a driver's prior comfort experiences, weather and route context to recommend clothing that suits that individual. This would require repeated observations and feedback from many drivers.

Implemented scope

Predict a shirt category

I narrowed the task to multiclass shirt classification using 34 engineered inputs and a tuned Random Forest. I independently prepared data, engineered features, evaluated the model and deployed the saved pipeline in Streamlit.

Original Streamlit prototype

Driver Gear Performance Lab

The application lets users enter route and weather conditions, load weather or use a manual scenario, and inspect a shirt recommendation, class probabilities and comparable historical training records.

Technical challenge

Recognizing data leakage

I struggled with models that could learn associations between clothing choices, such as pants and shirts. A related clothing field becomes a leakage risk if it reveals the target or is unavailable when the recommendation must be made. The deployed pipeline excludes leakage-prone post-outcome clothing fields and checks feature ordering.

View feature verification ↗

Methodological lesson

More tuning cannot replace evidence.

I learned that a small synthetic dataset could not establish how actual drivers respond to weather or support the full personalized system I envisioned. Narrowing the scope allowed me to deliver a working prototype without claiming a validated full-outfit recommendation.

05 / REFLECTION & FUTURE WORK

What I would do differently today.

The limitations of this first project helped me understand how technical skills, confidence and project scope influence the quality of an investigation.

Involve the people experiencing the problem. I did not formally involve fellow drivers or my delivery team in this project. At the time, I was still developing confidence in predictive analytics while balancing work and other classes. Looking back, I would have benefited from their perspectives and practical feedback.

Manage ambition through scope. I now understand that a broader solution requires a stronger data collection process, a carefully defined target and enough time to evaluate it properly. I would be more comfortable undertaking that challenge today, but I would still approach it in stages.

Future / 01

Gather real driver observations.

With voluntary participation, record clothing choices, shift conditions, route characteristics and post-shift comfort feedback across multiple drivers and changing seasons.

Future / 02

Evaluate personalization.

Define what is known before each shift, distinguish inputs from outcomes, and test how recommendations generalize to new shifts and drivers.

Future / 03

Rethink the application.

Develop a clearer interface for personal clothing inventories, changing conditions and practical alternatives. Test recommendations with genuine user feedback before expanding beyond shirts.

WHAT TO KEEP IN MIND

The modeling data is synthetic; reported test metrics measure an internal holdout, not verified performance on real-world driver decisions. Personalized outfit recommendations, multi-driver validation and the proposed interface improvements are future work, not existing capabilities.
