01 / THE QUESTION

A forecast isn't just a number.

Electricity demand follows familiar rhythms. But a model that performs well on an average day might behave very differently during the highest-demand hours.

We studied next-day hourly load forecasting in the ERCOT region of Texas. Sarah prepared the demand and feature data. Andrea developed the forecasting approaches. I examined the resulting predictions visually, compared their errors, and looked for relationships the summary scores could hide.

That led me to a question I wanted to test: if two models make different kinds of mistakes, could combining their predictions improve the result? The team evaluated a fixed 50/50 blend of Rolling SARIMA and Weather XGBoost. The result was useful, but it also revealed why an average accuracy score is not the entire story.

Scope of the studyThe research uses historical EIA-930 demand and team forecast exports. The test covers October 2025 through September 2026; 8,640 hourly records have all forecast series available for a matched comparison.

01 / THE BENCHMARK

Start by comparing against ERCOT.

Among our individual models, Weather XGBoost was strongest on the shared sample. Our later 50/50 hybrid improved further. But ERCOT's published day-ahead forecast still had the lowest overall error. That benchmark keeps the conclusions in perspective.

02 / THE PEAK HOURS

Average performance can hide a weakness.

At the highest observed demand levels, Rolling SARIMA and Weather SARIMAX were far more accurate than the tree-based approaches. We couldn't call the hybrid the best choice for every situation simply because it won on overall MAE.

Peak-hour analysis: 88 matched hours above the observed 99th-percentile cutoff

03 / THE EXPERIMENT

Blending helped, but not everywhere.

The simple 50/50 average of Rolling SARIMA and Weather XGBoost achieved the lowest overall MAE among our approaches. Its error rose in the top-demand slice, showing how the benefit of combining forecasts depends on the conditions being evaluated.

Hybrid: 2,006 MWh overall MAE · 3,189 MWh at the highest 1% of demand

04 / THE RESEARCH WEBSITE

Explore how the evidence connects.

The video explains our conclusions in sequence. The website I created is there for a different reason: it lets someone move through the evidence in whatever order makes sense to them.

Instead of searching through a long notebook to find which figure supports a statement, visitors can start with a model result, follow its documented links, inspect the underlying figures or metrics, and read the caveats that qualify the interpretation.

How I would use it to review this project

  1. Start with a finding. Open the connection map and select the result you want to understand. For example, why the team model ranking changes during peak demand.
  2. Ask “Why linked?” Inspect the recorded relationship between a finding, its comparison figure, a calculated metric, or a limitation. The viewer explains why the connection exists.
  3. Follow the original evidence. Open the linked notebook figure, check the model score and its source, or use Trace a path to explore how the research records relate.
  4. Check what the claim cannot prove. Look at the scientific limitations before treating a retrospective forecast comparison as a ready-to-deploy operating system.

THE RESEARCH BOUNDARY

What these results do—and do not—show.

This is a retrospective study, not an operational forecasting deployment. Models do not necessarily use identical issue times or information available before the forecast hour. Some weather-enhanced experiments use observed historical temperatures, not advance weather predictions. The highest-demand 1% is identified using actual demand after the fact. Neither matched error scores nor a research graph prove causality or quantify prevented outages or financial savings.
